Family side project · 3D browser game

A monster game, built from a kid’s imagination.

My kid wanted a game starring his favourite monster—but not a generic one. So we sat down with paper, invented characters, powers, controls, and a Toronto setting, then turned those ideas into concept art and a real playable prototype with AI and code.

Why we made it

The brief came from the best kind of client.

He already knew the feeling he wanted: enormous monsters, a dark city, a siren blast, soldiers, and a final showdown beneath the CN Tower.

We didn’t begin at a keyboard. We sketched the world on paper and talked through what each character could do. Siren Head would sweep enemies aside, bite at close range, and unleash a sonic blast. House Head would move like a giant spider. The city needed rain, lightning, and landmarks that made the monsters feel impossibly tall.

The fun was not only finishing a game. It was showing how a rough idea can move through design, visual experimentation, engineering, and play-testing—and that a child’s imagination can be a legitimate product brief.

From paper to playable

Four loops, one small game.

Each step gave us something concrete to react to, change, and improve together.

  1. 01

    Imagine

    Characters, powers, controls, story beats, and the map started as conversations and paper sketches.

  2. 02

    Visualize

    We used generative AI to explore the look of Siren Head, House Head, and the stylized soldiers.

  3. 03

    Build

    AI-assisted coding helped translate the design into JavaScript, Three.js, procedural 3D models, combat, and enemy behaviour.

  4. 04

    Play-test

    We tuned movement, scale, attacks, lighting, weather, and difficulty by playing the prototype and iterating.

What this project taught me

AI shortened the distance between imagination and a first working version.

It did not replace design or judgment. We still had to decide what was fun, communicate the feel we wanted, break the problem into pieces, test what came back, and keep improving it. That made the build a small lesson in product thinking—and a memorable way to make something together.

  • Translate a playful idea into a scoped prototype
  • Use AI for exploration, not as a substitute for taste
  • Design for scale, feedback, and a clear five-minute loop
  • Ship something real enough for another person to play
